How twelve thousand satellites get screened

The same three steps a space-traffic team runs, on public data: propagate every orbit, sieve for pairs that come near each other, then measure each near miss precisely.

The data

Propagation

Each two-line element set is turned into a position and velocity at any moment with SGP4, the standard model these elements are published for. It includes Earth's flattening and a simple drag model. Typical error for public elements is around a kilometre, so treat any pass under that as "close enough to look at", not a certainty.

The sieve

Comparing every pair (73 million of them) at every moment is too slow. Instead, every 20 seconds all satellites are dropped into a 3-D grid of cubes about 175 km across. Only satellites in the same or neighbouring cubes are compared. For each such pair the straight-line relative motion over that 20 s gives the closest distance; pairs under three times the threshold go on for refinement. Pairs moving together at under 100 m/s, such as freshly launched batches, are counted as formation flying and set aside. The sieve was checked against a 5-second scan and missed nothing.

Refinement

For each candidate, a golden-section search on full SGP4 positions finds the time of closest approach to within milliseconds and the miss distance to within a centimetre of a brute-force scan. The miss is broken into radial, along-track and cross-track parts in the first satellite's frame.

Risk and the burn planner

Collision probability needs the orbit uncertainty, which public elements don't carry. The figure shown assumes 1 km of uncertainty per object and a combined size of 10 m, so read it as a ranking aid. The burn planner uses Clohessy–Wiltshire equations: a push along the flight direction raises the orbit and, over time, slides the satellite along its track by about three times the push multiplied by the time before the pass.

The one-satellite check

The check runs the same sieve in a background thread in your browser, limited to objects whose perigee and apogee overlap the chosen satellite's, which is what keeps it fast.
